from telnet to the web

Nobody browsed to these rooms. You connected to them. In the telnet era the road in was a modem, a phone line, and a remote login: telnet — a protocol developed for ARPANET in 1969 and formalized in RFC 854 and RFC 855 in 1983 — opened a session on a distant computer, usually on port 23, presenting your terminal to it as a Network Virtual Terminal. Everything you typed traveled as clear text, passwords included; when SSH arrived in the late 1990s, it displaced telnet for exactly that reason. A period account of reaching TMN, preserved in the sister edition’s wiki, catches the whole ritual in one line: “I simply type ‘telnet tmn.com’, and instantly I am logged in to the Meta Network and given a list of open conferences and topics.”

The meter ran while you read. Charles Roth remembers paying seven to fifteen dollars an hour, by long-distance phone or via Telenet, to reach a distant computer at 300 baud; EIES charged seventy-five dollars a month plus connect time. Conferencing’s manners — read what is new, answer in order, sign your name, do not waste the room’s time — are the manners of people paying by the hour to be thoughtful in public. The era before even that — computer time bought on mainframes by the hour, output on thermal printers — predates the editor of this edition (“way pre-me,” in his words); in 1994 he saw its remnants, and heard its stories, in the Arlington offices where TMN ran: the previous computing age still standing in the rooms of the new one. His own arrival online belongs to that pre-web landscape: he came on in 1993, by ClarkNet dial-up, in the PNI year — “It was Gopher, too early for web when I got on,” in his words. The web transition below happened around him, in 1994–96, not before him — and he watched it arrive from inside the trade: at PNI’s Fairfax image lab, on Solaris boxes — a T1 line and X Windows, as he recalls the setup — he was there, in his words, “when Mosaic and port 80 became a thing.” His email at the lab, he believes — the hedge is his own: “I think it was” — ran under SRA’s media subdomain, media.sra.com. That subdomain is receipted in period traffic: a Usenet attic file carries a message from it dated April 13, 1994, squarely in his PNI window. No header bearing his own address has been sighted, so the address itself prints here as his recollection, hedged exactly as he hedged it.

The web changed the road, not the room. ARTS WIRE moved to the web in 1996; Screen Porch rebuilt Caucus so that by 1998 “the client needs only a common Web browser”; tmn.com carried web pages alongside the conferences; The WELL today is reached by SSH or the web. What the browser dissolved was the threshold: no login ritual, no meter running, no amber screen — and, in time, the room itself, as the feed replaced the conference. This edition keeps both thresholds in view: its cmd face is the telnet-era screen; its web face is the work’s 2000 re-housing; the snapshot underneath is the same.
